## What we are solving

Three niches looked plausible at the same time, and I had nothing to order them by. Each carried its own phrase, its own market and no reason at all to prefer it to the one beside it.

So I ran all three through one instrument on one day — Semrush Keyword Overview, read on 2026-08-12, one database per market.

The rest of this page is that single pull. An estimate made from outside, and what it does not cover I wrote down before the procedure.

## Steps

### What this instrument is, and how to get at it

Semrush is a paid panel that estimates search demand. It reports how often a phrase is typed in a month, what a click on it costs, and how hard the top of the results would be.

The load-bearing word is estimates. It gets no data from Google directly. By its own documentation it buys results pages from third-party providers, reads the top hundred positions, and models the rest with its own algorithm. Volumes refresh anywhere from daily to monthly depending on how popular the phrase is.

Two things follow from that. A number in the panel is a model rather than a counter, so the database and the engine belong beside it. And the keyword databases are built on Google, which is why it sees Russian demand poorly — on my phrase it disagreed with Wordstat by a factor of eighteen.

So why use it. Because it puts volume, click price and advertiser count on one screen in one set of units. Assembling the same thing free from three separate places, then comparing across them, does not work.

Access works like this. A free account takes an email and no card. It gives you **10 queries a day** against the domain, keyword, competitor and backlink reports, which is enough to check three or four phrases once. The full panel opens with a **seven-day trial**. That one does want a card, cancels at any point, and rolls into a paid subscription if you forget.

Set a reminder for day six. Doing all the counting in one sitting is better anyway: reading every phrase on the same day is the only way the numbers stay comparable.

Do not buy access through a reseller. The account is shared, the limits are shared, and you are paying somebody with no relationship to the vendor.

### Three niches, one market

All three phrases read in the United States database, on the same day. This is how you open it — substitute your phrase and your database:

```
https://www.semrush.com/analytics/keywordoverview/?q=ai+voice+agent&db=us
```

| Phrase | Volume | CPC | Keyword Difficulty | Intent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `youtube transcript` | 60,500 | $0.86 | 70% | Navigational |
| `pdf to text` | 9,900 | $0.79 | 43% | Informational |
| `ai voice agent` | 1,900 | $16.18 | 40% | Informational |

Volume and money went in opposite directions here: `youtube transcript` has 32 times the searches of `ai voice agent`, and a click worth 19 times less.

The variation clusters do not rescue it. `youtube transcript` carries 4,900 keywords on 382,600 volume, `pdf to text` 15,900 on 247,600.

Intent decides what you can charge for at all. Navigational means the person is after one specific free tool, Informational means they want to work out how it is done, and neither of them buys a subscription.

The panel puts the commercial reading somewhere else entirely, and that is the next table but one.

Difficulty runs the same way: 70% on `youtube transcript` against 40% on `ai voice agent`. Semrush's note under that 70% asks for 126 high-authority referring domains, so the biggest niche of the three also turned out to be the slowest to enter.

### The same phrase, four markets

`ai voice agent` in three English-speaking databases, and the Russian equivalent `голосовой бот` in the Russian one.

| Market | Phrase | Volume | CPC | Keyword Difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| United States | `ai voice agent` | 1,900 | $16.18 | 40% |
| United Kingdom | `ai voice agent` | 260 | $9.75 | 48% |
| Germany | `ai voice agent` | 110 | $9.91 | 40% |
| Russia | `голосовой бот` | 390 | $2.65, no live auction | 64% |

The same idea pays $16.18 a click in the United States, $9.75 in the United Kingdom and $9.91 in Germany. Markets differ by a good deal more than the language on the page.

Three databases, one engine, one day. That part of the comparison held, and it is the part that picked the market.

The fourth row lives by different rules. Google stopped serving ads to users in Russia on 3 March 2022 and deactivated Russia-based publisher accounts in August 2024. So $2.65 is a modelled figure for an auction that does not run.

The panel says none of that. It hands you a filled-in field, in dollars, next to three markets where the same field means a live bid.

The rest of the Russian row survives, with a label on it. Volume, difficulty and the variation counts were read in Google only, and the larger part of that demand is served by Yandex.

The difficulty there is higher too, 64% against 40%. Only the score is relative to its own database, so those two numbers cannot sit side by side.

Inside the panel the Russian branch looks thinner: 376 variations on 2,700 total volume against 2,969 on 32,000 in English. The question block says the same — 51 questions on 110 volume in Russian, 181 on 1,200 in English.

This page used to read that as a market about a twelfth the size. Then I typed the same phrase into the other engine.

### Two phrasings of one idea

Word order changes the reading. Both rows come from the United States database, on the same day.

| Phrase | Volume | CPC | Keyword Difficulty | Competitive density | Intent |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `ai voice agent` | 1,900 | $16.18 | 40% | 0.52 | Informational |
| `voice ai agents` | 590 | $16.94 | 39% | 0.41 | Commercial |

The commercial reading sits on the smaller phrase, at the higher click, and which of the two I build the page around decides who turns up on it.

More reliable than any price here is the advertising. Fourteen advertisers are bidding on `ai voice agent` at once — fourteen people paying for that click with their own money, and not for the first month. The CPC beside them is a model's estimate.

### The long specific phrases

Inside the English branch sit phrases with real volume and almost no difficulty.

| Phrase | Volume | Keyword Difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| `ai voice agent restaurants australia` | 1,600 | 10 |
| `ai voice agent cold calling b2b austria gdpr legal` | 590 | 9 |

The first one carries more volume than the head phrase in the United Kingdom. I would not have invented either of them at my desk — they turned up only in the variations list sorted by difficulty, which is where to go looking for them.

### What these figures do not cover

- **Every number here is Semrush's estimate from outside**. It is not a measurement of anyone's property. Its one-day estimate and a ninety-day average from my property are different quantities: [what a paid rank tracker measures](/tools/paid-tools/).
- **Keyword Difficulty is scored against its own database**. So 40% and 64% are not strictly comparable across markets. Comparable within a market only.
- **Semrush does not cover Yandex**. For Russian-language demand that is a large hole, and this table is Google-only. On the one phrase I measured, the hole was a factor of eighteen.
- **The Russian CPC has no auction behind it**. Google has not served ads to users in Russia since March 2022. The field is filled in all the same.
- **The English rows come from the United States database**. The country split next to the global volume is volume only, not CPC.

### Which phrasings to take before the tool is open

Your own word, a near-synonym, and the word a competitor uses. One phrasing is a sample of one: `ai voice agent` and `voice ai agents` came back with different intent, and they were on the same screen, one row under the other.

### What to read in the row besides volume

Volume on its own is not worth reading; it goes next to intent and CPC. A headline of 60,500 at $0.86 and Navigational is a crowd looking for a free tool.

Then the Ads field and the live results page: `ai voice agent` shows Ads 14, `youtube transcript` shows n/a on 60,500 searches a month.

And as a separate question, whether the auction behind that price runs at all. A price needs bids and bids need a country. Google has not served ads to users in Russia since March 2022, and the `db=ru` panel returns a CPC in dollars anyway, without a single note on it.

### How not to put two instruments in one table

Re-read every phrase in its own market database, switching the country by hand rather than translating in your head. Write the database name next to every figure: a number without its database compares to nothing, and this is exactly where it burned me.

### How the choice ends

Name the market out loud, in one sentence, with a figure inside it. Mine came out as this: build for the United States, because the click is worth $16.18 and fourteen advertisers agree.

## What did not work

- **Picking the niche by search volume**. 60,500 looked like the goldmine of the three. That click is worth $0.86, the difficulty is 70%, and the intent is Navigational. I would have entered the slowest niche for the cheapest traffic.
- **Assuming the domestic-language market is the easier one**. The one panel I had cannot see Yandex, and the CPC it printed has no auction behind it. Whether that market is easier, this pull never had the instruments to say.
- **Sizing a market with the one panel that cannot see it**. This page said the Russian branch was about a twelfth of the English one, on Semrush's 390. Wordstat gives 7,034 for the same phrase in the same month. The figure I published described my instrument, and I put a caveat above it instead of deleting it.
- **Reading a CPC without asking whether the auction exists**. I put $2.65 next to $16.18 and called it a comparison of markets. Google has not served ads in Russia since March 2022, so one of those prices has nobody paying it.
- **Letting a filled-in field stand for a fact**. The panel had no empty cell to warn me with, and I read a populated column as a measured one. A field is filled because the model can produce a number.
- **Treating one phrasing as the whole niche**. I read `ai voice agent`, saw Informational, and nearly wrote the niche off. `voice ai agents` reads Commercial at $16.94, and it was one line further down the same screen.
- **Reading CPC without the ad count**. A price with nobody paying it is a forecast. Ads 14 is fourteen live bidders, and it is the field I checked last.
- **Comparing difficulty across databases as one scale**. I put 40% and 64% in a single row and drew a conclusion for a day. The score is relative to its own database, so that row compares nothing.
- **Reading the country split as a market list**. US 1,900, IN 880, UK 260, CA 170, AU 140, DE 110. The split is volume only, and India at 880 says nothing about what a click there costs.
- **Taking the panel for a measurement**. These are modelled figures about markets I have not entered. I once set its estimate beside a ninety-day Search Console average. The gap was two different quantities, not a verdict on either: [what a paid rank tracker measures](/tools/paid-tools/).

## Verify

- Write one row per phrase with all four fields in it: volume, intent, CPC, ads. A row missing a field is not a decision yet.
- Re-run each phrase in its own market database and put the database name next to the number. Two numbers without databases are not a comparison.
- Count the ads by hand on the live results page, on two separate days. One day of ads proves nothing about a budget.
- Name the engine behind every row. For Russian-language demand check Wordstat too, and treat that check as mandatory. On my one phrase the two engines were eighteen times apart — [the words people actually type](/demand/how-people-search/).
- State the market you picked in one sentence, with the figure that decided it. If the sentence works without the figure, you picked by taste.

In sum, the biggest number of the three was the worst niche of the three. Whether the audience behind the expensive click pays anything is the next question: [whether that audience pays for anything](/demand/will-they-pay/).
