People use the thing and nobody pays. You cannot say whether any price at all would cover the bills, because you have never counted what a single action costs you to serve.

If the product calls an AI on the user's behalf, every action already has a cost. The provider sends that cost back in the response. You either put it on the event row right then, or you lose it for good, because an hour later there is nowhere left to look it up.

So the arithmetic goes first. [What one user costs](/money/unit-economics/) is three numbers. What one action costs to serve. What a month costs in which nobody arrives. How many payers close that month — in whole people, not in percentages.

The same page shows how to measure that cost instead of estimating it. I lost months to this. I thought the column was there and empty, and it had never existed at all, so every figure I quoted that spring was reconstructed afterwards from a rates table.

You pick the model before you pick the price. [Per action or per month](/money/pricing-model/) is about how the two differ: somebody who comes back on their own buys a subscription, and somebody whose job is one-off buys a pack. Each model drags work behind it: a subscription drags brand and refunds.

Then comes the harder half, and here two pages disagree: the cost arithmetic keeps suggesting you shave the bills, while [getting the first person to pay](/money/first-payer/) says the bills were never the problem. Pick a number. Put the wall where it really stops somebody. Ask one person for money by hand.

A product nobody pays for and a product nobody has been asked to pay for look the same from here. Telling them apart is cheaper than another round of cost cutting. I did the cost cutting first.
