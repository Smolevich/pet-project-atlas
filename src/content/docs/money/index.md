People use the thing, nobody pays, and you cannot say whether any price at all would cover the bills. If the product calls an AI on the user's behalf, every action already has a cost attached to it, and that cost comes back in the provider's response. It either lands on the event row while the response is still in hand, or it is gone.

So the arithmetic goes first. [What one user costs](/money/unit-economics/) is the variable cost of one action, the fixed cost of a month in which nobody arrives, and break-even in whole payers instead of a percentage. It also covers measuring that cost rather than estimating it, which is where I lost months to a column nobody filled.

Before a price comes the model. [Per action or per month](/money/pricing-model/) covers how a subscription rests on people returning while pay-as-you-go rests on a one-off job. Each drags its own work behind it — for a subscription, brand and refunds.

Then the harder half, and the two pages disagree about where the work is. The model always suggests shaving costs; [getting the first person to pay](/money/first-payer/) says the denominator was never the problem. Pick a number, put the wall where it stops something, ask one person by hand.

A product nobody pays for and a product nobody has been asked to pay for look the same from here. Telling them apart is cheaper than another round of cost cutting, and I did the cost cutting first.
